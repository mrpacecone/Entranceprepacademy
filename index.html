⚖ Introduction to Law

Module 1 — Definitions, nature, characteristics of law and introduction to jurisprudence.

What is Law?

Law is a system of rules and regulations created and enforced by social or governmental institutions to regulate behaviour. Different jurisprudential schools define it differently.

Key Definitions

  • Austin: "Law is a command issued by a sovereign, backed by a sanction."
  • Salmond: "Law is the body of principles recognized and applied by the state in the administration of justice."
  • Holland: "Law is a general rule of external human action enforced by a sovereign political authority."
  • Roscoe Pound: Law is a means of social engineering to satisfy maximum wants with minimum friction.

Nature & Characteristics of Law

  • Normative: Law prescribes how people ought to behave
  • General: Applies to all persons without discrimination
  • Territorial: Operates within a definite territory
  • State-enforced: Backed by sovereign authority and sanctions
  • Dynamic: Law changes with society's needs

Types of Law

Civil vs Criminal: Civil governs disputes between private parties; criminal punishes acts that violate public order.

Constitutional Law: Supreme law establishing government structure and guaranteeing fundamental rights.

Administrative Law: Governs activities of government agencies.

Public vs Private: Public = state-individual relationship; Private = individual-individual relationship.

Substantive vs Procedural: Substantive defines rights & duties; procedural prescribes enforcement process.

Source: TU Faculty of Law Syllabus  |  Ref: meroadalat.com BALLB Syllabus Guide  |  Updated: 2025

🏛 Role of Law in Society

Module 2 — Law and social order, justice, development, rule of law, democracy and social change.

Law and Social Order

Law maintains social order by providing a framework of rules that regulate human behaviour and resolve conflicts. Without law, social cohesion would be impossible — Hobbes described the pre-legal state as "nasty, brutish and short."

Law and Justice

The primary aim of law is to achieve justice. Aristotle distinguished distributive justice (fair distribution of goods) and corrective justice (remedying wrongs). Formal justice = treating like cases alike.

Rule of Law

A.V. Dicey's three pillars: (1) Supremacy of law — no person is above law, (2) Equality before law, (3) Predominance of legal spirit — rights secured through ordinary courts. Enshrined in Article 1 of Nepal's Constitution 2015.

Law and Democracy

Democracy requires rule of law to function. Free and fair elections, protection of civil liberties, independent judiciary — all depend on a robust legal framework.

Social Change Through Law

Law can be an instrument of social change (Roscoe Pound's social engineering). Examples from Nepal: Muluki Ain reforms, caste discrimination laws, women's rights legislation.

High-Yield Points

  • Dicey's Rule of Law: 3 pillars — supremacy, equality, legal spirit
  • Aristotle: distributive vs corrective justice
  • Roscoe Pound: law as social engineering
  • Nepal: Art. 1 = constitutional supremacy = rule of law
Source: TU Faculty of Law Syllabus  |  Ref: CollegeNP BALLB Guide  |  Updated: 2025

📜 Sources of Law

Module 3 — Constitution, legislation, judicial precedents, customs, treaties and religious sources.

1. Constitution

The supreme source of law. All other laws must conform to it. In Nepal: Constitution of Nepal 2015 is the Grundnorm. Article 1 declares it the fundamental law — any inconsistent law is void.

2. Legislation

Primary legislation (Acts of Parliament) and subordinate/delegated legislation (Regulations, Rules, Bylaws made under delegated authority).

3. Judicial Precedents

Doctrine of stare decisis — higher court decisions bind lower courts. Nepal's Supreme Court decisions are binding precedents under Article 128(2) of the Constitution.

4. Custom

Long-established practices recognized as law. Must be: immemorial, reasonable, certain, consistent, compulsory, and peaceful. In Nepal, customs recognized under Muluki Civil Code 2017.

5. International Treaties

Ratified treaties become part of domestic law. Nepal is party to ICCPR, ICESCR, CEDAW, CRC, CAT and others. Article 278 of the Constitution governs treaty ratification.

6. Equity and Religious Sources

Equity fills gaps in common law — fairness and conscience. Religious sources (Hindu, Islamic law) historically significant but now secondary in Nepal's secular state framework.

Source: Constitution of Nepal 2015  |  TU Faculty of Law Syllabus  |  Updated: 2025

🤝 Rights & Duties

Module 4 — Natural rights, human rights, legal rights, civil rights, political rights and legal duties.

Types of Rights

  • Natural Rights: Inherent to all humans by nature — Locke: life, liberty, property. Pre-political and inalienable.
  • Human Rights: International standards under UDHR 1948. Universal, indivisible, interdependent.
  • Legal Rights: Rights recognized and enforceable by law. Arise from positive law.
  • Civil Rights: Right to equal treatment under law, non-discrimination.
  • Political Rights: Right to vote, stand for election, participate in governance.

Types of Duties

  • Moral Duties: Based on conscience and ethics — not legally enforceable (e.g., duty to help the poor).
  • Legal Duties: Obligations imposed by law and enforceable by state (e.g., duty to pay taxes).
  • Constitutional Duties: Article 48 of Nepal's Constitution — duties of citizens including defending sovereignty, paying taxes, environmental protection.

Salmond's Jural Relations (Hohfeld)

  • Right ↔ Duty (correlative)
  • Privilege ↔ No-right
  • Power ↔ Liability
  • Immunity ↔ Disability
Source: TU Faculty of Law Syllabus  |  Article 48, Constitution of Nepal 2015

👤 Person & Legal Personality

Module 5 — Natural persons, legal persons, legal capacity and liability.

Natural Person

A human being recognized by law as having legal rights and duties. Legal capacity begins at birth (with some rights recognised from conception). Ends at death.

Legal (Juristic) Person

Non-human entities granted legal personality: corporations, government bodies, registered institutions, universities, trusts. They can sue/be sued, own property, make contracts.

Theories of Legal Personality

  • Fiction Theory (Savigny): Legal persons are legal fictions — only humans are real persons.
  • Realist Theory (Gierke): Corporations have real existence, not mere fiction.
  • Concession Theory: Legal personality is granted by State — it's a concession.
  • Purpose Theory (Brinz): Legal person = a purpose or fund dedicated to a purpose.

Legal Capacity

Capacity to have rights (all persons) vs capacity to act (limited for minors under 18, mentally incapacitated). In Nepal: Muluki Civil Code 2017 governs capacity.

Liability

Civil liability: Breach of duty causing harm → compensation. Criminal liability: Requires actus reus (guilty act) + mens rea (guilty mind).

Source: Muluki Civil Code 2017  |  TU Faculty of Law Syllabus  |  Updated: 2025

📖 Constitution of Nepal

Module 6 — Constitutional development, organs of state, separation of powers and checks & balances.

Constitution of Nepal 2015 (2072 BS)

Promulgated on 20 September 2015 (3 Asoj 2072 BS). Nepal's seventh constitution. Replaced the Interim Constitution 2007.

Key Features

  • Federal Democratic Republic with 3 tiers of government
  • 7 Provinces, 77 Districts, 753 Local Bodies
  • 35+ Fundamental Rights guaranteed (Parts 2 & 3)
  • Independent judiciary with Supreme Court at apex
  • Bicameral federal parliament: House of Representatives (275) + National Assembly (59)
  • Constitutional Supremacy — Article 1
  • Secular state — Article 4

Constitutional Development Timeline

  • 1948: Government of Nepal Act (first constitution)
  • 1951: Interim Government of Nepal Act
  • 1959: Constitution of Nepal (multiparty democracy, first elected parliament)
  • 1962: Constitution of Nepal (Panchayat system — parties banned)
  • 1990: Constitution of the Kingdom of Nepal (parliamentary democracy restored)
  • 2007: Interim Constitution of Nepal (Republic declared in 2008)
  • 2015: Constitution of Nepal (current — federal, secular, republican)

Organs of State

Legislature: Bicameral Parliament — House of Representatives (275 members, 5-year term) + National Assembly (59 members, 6-year term). Lawmaking power.

Executive: President (constitutional head) + Prime Minister (executive head leading Council of Ministers). President elected by Electoral College.

Judiciary: Supreme Court → High Courts (7, one per province) → District Courts (77). Ensures rule of law and protects fundamental rights via judicial review.

Separation of Powers & Checks and Balances

Montesquieu's doctrine: legislative, executive, judicial powers separated to prevent tyranny. Nepal implements checks: Parliament can pass vote of no-confidence; PM advises dissolution; Supreme Court exercises judicial review over laws and executive acts.

Source: Constitution of Nepal 2015  |  Ref: nlc.edu.np  |  Updated: 2025

👨‍⚖️ Jurists & Legal Theories

Major jurists and their legal theories — repeatedly tested in BALLB entrance. Master all 9.

A
John Austin
Analytical / Positivist School
Command Theory: Law is a command of the sovereign backed by sanction. Strict separation of law and morality. Father of Analytical Jurisprudence.
S
John Salmond
Analytical School
Law as body of principles recognized by the state in administration of justice. Focused on rights, duties, persons and property.
P
Roscoe Pound
Sociological School
"Law is Social Engineering." Law serves to balance competing individual and social interests with maximum efficiency and minimum friction.
K
Hans Kelsen
Pure Theory of Law
Law as "normative science." Grundnorm (basic norm) at apex of hierarchy. Hierarchy of norms. Strict separation from morality and politics.
H
H.L.A. Hart
Neo-Positivist School
Primary rules (obligations) + Secondary rules (recognition, change, adjudication). Rule of Recognition. Critiqued Austin's Command Theory. "Concept of Law" (1961).
Sv
Friedrich Savigny
Historical School
Law grows organically with the nation's spirit (Volksgeist). Law is not made but found. Evolved from customs and traditions of the people.
B
Jeremy Bentham
Utilitarian School
Greatest happiness of the greatest number. Law as instrument of utility. Father of modern jurisprudence and legal positivism. Created panopticon.
Ho
Thomas Hobbes
Natural Law / Social Contract
Social contract theory. State of nature: "nasty, brutish and short." Sovereign power is absolute. Leviathan (1651). Predecessor to Austin's Command Theory.
M
Karl Marx
Marxist / Economic School
Law as superstructure of the economic base. Law serves the ruling class. Withering away of law under communism. Economic relations determine legal relations.

SubjectiveQ2. What is the Rule of Law? Explain its significance in Nepal. (10 marks)2024
Model Answer:

Definition: Rule of Law means supremacy of law over arbitrary power. A.V. Dicey identified three pillars: (1) Supremacy of law — no person punishable except for breach of law established by ordinary court; (2) Equality before law; (3) Predominance of legal spirit — rights result from judicial decisions.

Nepal Context: Article 1 of Nepal's Constitution 2015 declares it the fundamental law. Article 18 guarantees equality. The Supreme Court's power of judicial review (Art. 133) ensures rule of law over legislative and executive actions.

Significance: Prevents arbitrariness, protects fundamental rights, ensures accountability of state actors, enables predictable legal environment for development.

Creative WritingQ3. Write an essay on "Human Rights and their Importance in a Democratic Society" (~300 words, 20 marks)2024
Model Essay:

Human rights are fundamental entitlements that every person possesses by virtue of being human, irrespective of nationality, religion, race or status. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) first codified these rights globally, followed by binding treaties including the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR).

In a democratic society, human rights form the cornerstone of governance. They protect individuals from state abuse, ensure equal participation in political life, and guarantee the dignity of every person. Without human rights, elections would be meaningless and governance arbitrary. Democratic institutions — parliament, courts, press — exist to protect and promote these rights.

Nepal's Constitution 2015 enshrines over 35 fundamental rights, among the most comprehensive in South Asia. These include the right to equality (Art. 18), freedom (Art. 17), education (Art. 31), health (Art. 35), and constitutional remedies (Art. 46). The National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) monitors compliance.

Without human rights, democracy becomes hollow. A true democracy must not only proclaim rights but actively protect and enforce them through independent institutions. Nepal's journey — from monarchy to federal republic — illustrates that human rights and democracy are inseparable foundations of a just society.

When you host this on GitHub Pages, every student who visits the link stores their session data in their own browser's localStorage. Since this is a static site, you cannot see others' data directly.

To collect analytics from students:

  • Ask students to screenshot their "My Progress" and "Session Stats" and share with you
  • Or integrate a free analytics service: add Google Analytics or Plausible script to the <head> for real page-view tracking
  • The Admin Panel tracks your own sessions across visits to this URL
  • For multi-user tracking from a shared link, a backend server (Firebase, Supabase free tier) would be needed

Current session data shows your own usage history across visits to this site.
